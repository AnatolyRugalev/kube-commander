## Why this is a split and not a big leg

The board's THEME-04 was two palettes, a decision about terminals that filter the escape,
a retune of the darkness guard, two redundant guards dropped and two documents. That is
comfortably past a leg, and the two halves have a natural order that D249 pt 4 already
fixed: *decide what kubecom does when it cannot paint, then land the values*. So 04a is the
mechanism and 04b is the palettes.

The objection to that order is that a mechanism whose only consumer is a palette that does
not exist yet is speculative work, and D68 says a leg should improve the running binary.
It does not apply here, because **the mismatch is live today in the other direction**: all
eleven built-ins are dark, and a reader whose terminal is light and whose multiplexer
filters the escape is looking at dark text on a light background right now. This leg fires
for them, unchanged, with no light palette anywhere.

## The three options, and why two of them are wrong

D249 pt 4 named refuse / warn / detect.

**Refuse** — decline to apply a palette whose polarity cannot be guaranteed — overrides an
explicit `theme:` line on the strength of a guess, which is the opposite of principle 3 and
of D169's "degrade loudly, never silently pick". It also cannot be implemented honestly:
refusing requires knowing the request will fail, and the only thing kubecom can know is
what the terminal reports *after* the fact.

**Warn always** — say something whenever a light palette is selected — is a toast on a
perfectly good screen at every launch for everyone whose terminal honours the request,
which is most people. A warning that is usually wrong is a warning people learn to skip,
and it would still be there when the real one fires.

**Detect** is available and cheap, and this is the part worth writing down because it is
easy to miss: `tea.RequestBackgroundColor` sends OSC 11 as a *query*, and the terminal's
reply arrives as `tea.BackgroundColorMsg`. Bubble Tea has had this the whole time — it is
documented for the opposite use (pick your styles from the terminal's polarity). Pointed at
kubecom's own canvas it answers the exact question: is the screen I am drawing on the one I
asked for?

The rejected fourth option is worth naming so nobody reaches for it: sniffing `$TMUX`,
`$TERM` or `$TERM_PROGRAM` against a list of terminals believed to filter OSC 11. Such a
list is wrong the day it is written, wrong differently after every terminal release, and
silently wrong — which is the worst of the three properties.

## The probe is late, and that is a fix rather than a hedge

The first version probed immediately from `Init`. The renderer emits the background *set*
during the first paint and the query is a Cmd, so the two race: the query can leave before
the set, and the reply is then the terminal's *previous* background. On a terminal that
honours the request, that is a false "it did not land" — the mechanism reporting a failure
it caused itself.

So the probe is a `tea.Tick` of 750 ms. That was chosen over the alternatives deliberately:
there is no "first frame painted" message to hang it on, and a fast probe plus a retry
doubles the machinery for an answer that is only ever used to decide whether to print one
sentence. The pty run measures the result — the set at byte 342, the query at 362 — which
is what the ordering claim rests on, rather than on reading the renderer.

Two guards keep a switch honest. `canvasGen` retires the departed palette's tick (the same
stale-tick shape `seqGen` and `statusErrGen` use), and `awaitingCanvas` gates the *answer*,
because terminals do report their background unprompted and an unsolicited report says
nothing about whether kubecom's request landed. `applyThemeNamed` bumps one and clears the
other before arming the new probe.

## The silences are the design

Only one of four outcomes speaks:

| Terminal reports | kubecom |
|---|---|
| the palette's own canvas | silent — the request landed |
| a different colour, same polarity | silent — did not land, does not matter |
| nothing at all | silent — unknown is not evidence |
| a different colour, opposite polarity | one line |

The second row is the one that carries the weight. It is the ordinary case for all eleven
built-ins on a terminal that ignores OSC 11 — the way kubecom rendered for its whole life
before D249 — and a check that fired there would put a toast on a working screen at every
launch, for everybody, forever. Widening the rule to "warn whenever the colours differ" is
the single change that would ruin this, which is why D250 pt 3 states it as a constraint
rather than describing the code.

The third row matters for a different reason: a sandbox pty answers nothing, so silence is
also what CI and every scripted run see. A mechanism whose failure mode is "say nothing"
degrades in the right direction.

## What the line says, and what it does not

> `gruvbox-dark is dark but the terminal stayed light — background not applied`

It names the palette, the two polarities and the consequence. It does **not** say "tmux is
filtering the escape", because kubecom genuinely cannot tell a terminal that filtered the
request from one that declined it, and a confident wrong cause sends the reader to fix the
wrong thing. The remedies — `set-option -g allow-passthrough on`, pick a matching palette,
change the terminal — go into the log alongside both hex values, which is `surfaceError`'s
own split (D159): the toast is clipped to the width and gone in five seconds, the log line
is neither.

Nothing is disabled. The theme the user asked for still applies.

## Polarity is measured, not stored

The obvious move is `Theme.Dark bool`. It is a second copy of a fact the colors already
state, and the copy drifts the first time someone retunes a background without touching the
flag — a palette that measures light while declaring itself dark, which is exactly the
error the whole leg exists to catch.

So `luminance.go` exports the WCAG arithmetic the test file already had, and `IsDark` uses
`darkLuminance = 0.2` — *the registry's own admission threshold* from D248 pt 1. That
sharing is the point: "dark enough to be admitted to the registry" and "dark" are now one
number and cannot disagree, so THEME-04b cannot retune the guard into admitting a palette
that this code then describes to the user as the other polarity. `themes_test.go` calls the
exported functions rather than keeping its copies.

`SameColor` compares at 8 bits because the two sides come from different places: a Theme's
value is a hex literal, while a terminal answers OSC 11 in whatever precision it likes —
`rgb:28/28/28` and `rgb:2828/2828/2828` are the same colour said two ways, and comparing
the interface values or the raw 16-bit channels would call them different and warn.

## Four mutations

1. Return before the warn in `checkCanvas` → `TestOppositePolarityIsReported` fails; the
   three silence tests still pass, which is the point of having both kinds.
2. Drop the `awaitingCanvas` gate → `TestUnsolicitedBackgroundReportIsIgnored` fails.
3. Remove the bump/clear from `applyThemeNamed` → `TestThemeSwitchReArmsTheProbeAndRetires
   TheOldOne` fails on both halves: the gate is left open and the departed palette's answer
   is graded against the new one.
4. Accept a stale tick (drop the `gen` check) → `TestStaleCanvasProbeIsDropped` fails.

## What a test could not claim, and what was done instead

Two things, and only one of them stayed unproven.

The tests cannot show that the query leaves the process or that the ordering holds, so a
pty run measured the bytes. And they cannot show the end-to-end round trip, because the
model never talks to a terminal — so a second harness forked a pty, watched for
`\x1b]11;?` and wrote an OSC 11 reply back into the master fd. Answering `rgb:fbfb/f1f1/
c7c7` (gruvbox-light's own bg0) under `theme: gruvbox-dark` produced the line on screen and
the WARN in the log; answering `rgb:2828/2828/2828` produced neither. That technique is
recorded in `knowledge/themes.md`, because `script -qec` — what the last two THEME legs
used — cannot do it: it never answers.

What remains is a real terminal and a real multiplexer, and it went into the existing
`2026-08-08-app-background-look` task as **item 7** rather than a new file, since items 1-6
already ask the maintainer to sit down in front of exactly that setup. It asks two things,
and the second is the one that would make THEME-04b wrong: whether the line ever appears on
a screen that looks fine. It stays advisory and blocks nothing — a false-positive toast
would be a bug to fix, not a reason to hold the light palettes.

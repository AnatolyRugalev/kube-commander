## The reporter's diagnosis was wrong and the report was right

The feedback says esc "clears the palette's filter input instead, and the palette/menu
stays open". Nothing was typed in the repro, so the filter branch (`picker.Update`'s
ActionBack, non-empty query → `filter.Reset()`) never ran. What ran was the arm in
`app.go`: `palArg != ""` → `showPaletteVerbs()`. The palette stayed up because the leg that
wrote that line meant it to.

From the reader's chair the two are indistinguishable — a modal that does not close, with
something changing inside it — and the observation ("esc doesn't let me back out") was
exactly right. Worth recording because the obvious fix suggested by the wording is to
change the picker's filter behaviour, which is shared by every picker in the app and would
have broken the `/`-search habit in five other places while leaving this bug alone.

## Why D207 pt 2 was wrong only for half of what it said

D207 pt 2 is one sentence covering two gestures: *"Esc and backspace rewind to the verb
list before they close."* Its justification is the good one — the rewind is what makes a
shortcut key "a way into the palette rather than a faster dead end", so pressing the wrong
one leaves you a keystroke from every other verb. That argument survives this leg
completely, and it never needed esc to carry it.

The split is: **backspace edits the line, esc leaves the surface.** `:action ` is a line
with a committed word in it; backspacing that word off is literally what backspace does to
a line, and the verb list appearing is the line's own previous state. Esc is the app's
universal back — out of the logs view, out of a modal, out of a search — and back means
*where you were*. Someone who pressed `a` was on the pod table. Sending them to the verb
list is not backing out; it is a lateral move that still owes them an esc.

That is also why the fix is a flag on the *stage's provenance* rather than a special case
per key. `enterPaletteArg` cannot know: it is one function precisely so a typed stage and a
key-opened stage are the same object (D207 pt 1), and that is worth keeping. The caller
knows, so `openPaletteArg` is the only thing that sets `palDirect`, and every other route
through the palette clears it. A door added later has to answer the question by
construction — it either goes through `openPaletteArg` or it does not.

## The tests moved rather than died, and that is what caught the scope

Three tests failed on the first run, and they were exactly the three predicted:
`TestThemeStageCancelRewindsThenCloses`, `TestNamespacePickerCancels`,
`TestResourceStageCancelRewindsThenCloses` — one per converted key that had an esc test.
Each is now two tests instead of one (`…CancelClosesOutright` + `…BackspaceRewinds`),
which is D207 pt 4's rule applied to a behaviour change rather than a refactor: the
guarantee that made the key worth converting is still pinned, it is just pinned on the
gesture that still provides it. Deleting the rewind assertions along with the esc ones
would have quietly dropped the property D207 pt 1 exists for.

The interesting one is the *fourth* test, which did not fail: `TestLateContextListIsDropped`
sends two `CancelledMsg`s and asserts the palette is closed. With the change, the first
closes and the second is a no-op on an already-closed palette, so it passed while its
comment ("rewinds to the verbs" / "then closes") had become false. That is the shape
BOARD-02b-3 kept finding in the board's prose, and it turns out tests grow it too: an
assertion about the end state survives a change to the path, and takes its stale narration
with it. Fixed to one cancel.

## The query call, made explicitly because the feedback asked for it

The report offered a fork: *"If a filter is typed and the menu should stay open on the
first Esc, that's a product call to make explicitly."* D233 pt 3 makes it: the query keeps
its esc. Two reasons. It is what esc does to an input field everywhere else, including this
app's own `/` search, so changing it here would make the palette the exception. And it is
the same ordering as pt 1 — unwind the innermost visible thing the reader made, then leave
— which is the rule that produced the fix, not a carve-out from it.

Worth being honest about the cost: from a typed action stage, leaving still takes two escs.
The difference from before is that the second one now *leaves*, rather than depositing you
on a verb list to spend a third.

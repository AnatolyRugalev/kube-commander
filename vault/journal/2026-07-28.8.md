- Did: Took the last SEARCH slice. `kube.Search`'s name matcher now falls back to a
  subsequence match when the contiguous pass finds nothing — `apisrv` finds `api-server`,
  `kdns` finds `kube-dns` — scored in a band strictly below every substring hit, and the

- **The cap decision, which the board flagged and left open.** It offered two answers:
  fall back to subsequence only when a kind's substring pass found nothing, or make the cap
  score-aware. The first is the wrong shape and it took writing it out to see why — it
  bounds nothing. A kind with zero substring matches still emits *every* scattered match it
  has, which on a loose query is most of its rows, so one unlucky kind can still burn the
  whole cap before a good kind returns. Worse, it makes a row's fate depend on *other rows
  in the same kind*: the same pod is shown or hidden according to whether some unrelated
  pod in the same namespace happened to match exactly. So: a budget. Scattered hits get
  `limit/4` and no more, checked under the same lock that makes the cap exact.
- **The budget is not a second cap, and the difference is the whole design.** Exhausting it
  drops the hit and nothing else — the sweep is *not* cancelled (only the real cap does
  that), the kind keeps being scanned for contiguous matches, and `SearchDone.Capped` stays
  false. That last one is a judgement: `Capped` drives `first 200 matches — narrow the
  query`, and telling a reader to narrow because some fuzzy near-misses were withheld sends
  them after nothing. The asymmetry is deliberate and is the rule worth keeping (D153 pt 3):
  substring hits may fill the entire cap and starve fuzzy completely, which is correct;
  fuzzy may never starve substring.
- **A test I wrote to confirm the design instead falsified it.** I gave the scattered band
  the same shape as the substring band — position bonuses on top, span as a clamped
  tie-breaker — and then asserted that a tight match after a separator beats one strewn
  across a name from index 0. It failed 388 to 576, and the failure was right: a 400-point
  start bonus swamps a 50-point span clamp, so any name whose first letter happens to be the
  query's first letter outranks a genuinely tight match. That is exactly the noise the band
  exists to sink, admitted at the top of the band.
  The fix is D153 pt 2: **the two bands weigh different things.** A contiguous match has a
  fixed span — position is the only thing left to read. A scattered match's span varies, so
  *tightness* is the signal and the positional bonuses do not apply at all; position enters
  only through the clamped start penalty. It is tempting to unify the two formulas; they
  answer different questions and only the band gap is shared.
- **The two greedy passes, and the reason the second one pays.** Forward-greedy (each needle
  character at its earliest position) finds *a* window but often a needlessly wide one:
  `abc` in `a-zz-b-zz-ab.c` matches across the whole name before reaching the tight `ab.c`
  at the end. Walking back from where the forward pass ended pulls the window as far right
  as it goes. What makes this worth doing is subtle: shifting the window right by *n* costs
  *n* in start penalty and saves *n* in gaps, so with equal weights the two windows score
  **identically** and the backtrack is pure decoration. Weighting gaps at 2× is what makes
  the tightest window strictly best — and it is also why there is no need to score both
  candidates and take the max, the way the contiguous pass does over its occurrences.
  There is a test that inverts if the backtrack is removed.
- **`scattered` is returned from the matcher, not derived from the score.** Tempting to say
  "below the substring band ⇒ fuzzy" and skip the extra return value, but an empty-needle
  hit (a pure `-l` label query) also scores 0, and charging those to the fuzzy budget would
  cap a label-selector search at 50 results for no reason. A test pins it.
- **No new syntax, no new key, no hint change.** Fuzzy has nothing to turn on, so unlike
  `-l` (which needed the blank hint to teach it) there is nothing a reader could fail to
  discover — they type what they always typed and get more back. The blank hint is left
  alone rather than lengthened to boast.
- Decisions: D153 — a widened matcher is a fallback that can only add results below the
  existing ones, never re-rank them; bands may (and should) weigh their terms differently,
  with only the band gap shared; an emit-time cap that ranks downstream must budget by match
  quality, and a stricter match kind may starve a looser one but never the reverse.
